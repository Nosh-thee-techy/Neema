The problem

02

Kenya loses a mother about every hour and a half.

~5,700

women died from pregnancy and childbirth in 2023: about 15 a day

379

deaths per 100,000 live births. The 2030 target is under 70.

2 in 5

maternal deaths reviewed involved heavy bleeding

~1 in 5

new mothers in a Nairobi study showed signs of postpartum depression

And the danger doesn’t end at birth: most deaths from bleeding come on day 1, from high blood pressure in week 1, and from infection in weeks 2 to 6.

UN Trends in Maternal Mortality 2000–2023 (via Business Daily); Kenya Confidential Enquiry into Maternal Deaths (via Nation); BMC Psychiatry 2018; Dol et al., JBI Evidence Synthesis 2022. “15 a day” = 5,700 ÷ 365.

Before birth · Wanjiku’s story

03

Wanjiku lost her first pregnancy last year.

Now she is ten weeks pregnant again, and afraid. How do we make sure she is watched closely this time?

Wanjiku is a composite story. Neema cannot promise a loss won’t happen again; it makes sure no warning sign waits until morning.

Week 10

Her health promoter registers her on Neema and records the loss. Neema never calls her “high-risk”. It keeps her past in mind, quietly.

Every day

A one-minute check-in, by tap or by voice in Kiswahili. The band learns her own usual heartbeat.

Every visit

Today shows her next clinic visit, so none is missed. The nurse scans her summary from her phone.

Week 16

Bleeding and strong tummy pain one evening. Neema turns RED: “Go to a health facility now. Do not wait for morning.” An SMS with her location is ready.

From week 28

“Baby moving less” means the clinic today, never tomorrow.

How Neema watches over her pregnancy

04

With her every day, not only on the bad one.

On her wrist

The Neema band

Feels her heartbeat and skin temperature day and night. An SOS button, a light and a buzz.

Danger signs

Never “wait and see”. Bleeding, severe tummy pain, fits, fainting: red at once. Fever or a high reading: check again today.

Her readings

Blood pressure in plain words, never pre-filled. A raised reading with a headache is red.

One clear answer

GREENYELLOWREDUNKNOWN

Calm, check again, get help now. Unsure is never shown as calm.

Two seconds to help

Hold the button: the phone calls 999. SMS to her health promoter and family, with where she is. No answer? The next person. Only a health worker can mark her reached.

After birth · the danger no cuff can measure

06

Week five. She smiles for customers. Nobody asks how she really is.

Nearly 1 in 5 new mothers in a Nairobi study, and 27% in Nairobi’s informal settlements, showed signs of postpartum depression.

Every day

A few taps in “Sleep & mood”: how she slept, if she rested, how she feels.

Week 5

Low mood on several days. Neema: “That is common, and help works,” and offers the mood questions in Kiswahili.

Same day

“This is not a diagnosis. It is support.” Talk to a person; support lines one tap away.

Not alone

A mothers’ circle where she writes under a cartoon name, never her own. If she thinks of harm, a person is reached now. The SMS says only: she needs someone to talk to.

Composite story. BMC Psychiatry 2018 (18.7%, two Nairobi public hospitals); BMC Psychiatry 2022 (27.1%, Lang’ata and Riruta clinics).

Next · Wanjiku’s own watch plan

A past loss: from 28 weeks, asked every day whether her baby moves as usual. Bleeding at the birth: heartbeat watched closely for 6 weeks. A slow climb from 86 to 110 stayed green before; with her plan it turns yellow at 2:30 pm, and her promoter is told why. Another mother, with high blood pressure: a reading every 2 days.

Watch plan: built and tested as a prototype, being redesigned before release; the 2:30 pm result is from the team’s simulation. Every limit is a draft for clinicians to set.

Inclusive by design

08

No mother left out because of how she lives.

Her language, her voice

If she reads little English: Kiswahili and English, read aloud. She can speak her answers instead of typing.

Signs, too

If she is deaf: an early sign-language mode, so no mother hears about danger last.

Works offline

If she has no data bundle: readings and checks need no internet. Alerts go by SMS and reach any phone, even a mulika mwizi.

Set up with her

If she is new to smartphones: her health promoter or nurse registers her, step by step. Big, clear buttons.

Point to it

If she can’t name the pain: she taps where it hurts on a picture of a woman’s body.

Private, never labelled

If she fears being judged: kept on her phone behind her PIN. Her nurse scans a summary; nothing goes online.

29.6 million of Kenya’s 78.3 million mobile phones were basic feature phones in December 2025 (Communications Authority of Kenya, via The Star).
